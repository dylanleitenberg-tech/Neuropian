In plain English

Three steps.

  1. 1

    Look at real work.

    Code that experts accepted into major software, results from programming contests, answers to hard math questions. All of it public.

  2. 2

    Skip the famous.

    Remove anyone a recruiter can already find: people with big online followings, jobs at big companies, or a leading role in their project.

  3. 3

    Show the proof.

    Write a short report on each person, with links to the actual work, so an employer can check it for themselves.

What is left: people whose work has been checked by experts, but whom few people follow.

Method · numbers as of 17–18 Oct 2026

Two kinds of evidence, one report.

01

Reasoners

Competitive programmers and mathematicians. Neuropian reads their contest results and math answers, and among people with the same score it is better at spotting who will reach the top level next.

1.5×the predictive signal of the score alone, for spotting who reaches the top level (a 2400 rating) among Codeforces players with the same score. Tested on two later years the system had never seen.
Method and numbers
  • Cohort. Every Codeforces player rated 1600–2300 on 16 Oct 2024, with at least 15 rated contests and 3 in the year before: 11,088 players. 290 (2.6%) reached 2400 in the two years after.
  • Chosen on earlier data. Same rules on 16 Oct 2022: 9,305 players, 274 reached 2400 by Oct 2024, before the test period starts. Traits kept if their within-band AUC was above 0.55, reversed if below 0.45: 12-month rating gain, how few contests it took to reach 2000 (0 if never), months with a contest in the 12 months before their last one, distance below their own peak, minus calendar years with a rated contest.
  • Ranking, also chosen on 2022–24. Candidates: rating alone; rating plus 0.05, 0.1 or 0.2 × score; rating bands of 10, 25, 50 or 100 points with the score ordering each band. 25-point bands found the most future 2400s in the top 50, 100 and 290.
  • Test. Frozen and applied to the 2024 cohort: the score alone gets 0.647 within 100-point bands (rating: 0.599) and 0.632 within 10-point bands. The deployed ranking gets AUC 0.899 overall, against 0.896 for rating alone (95% CI 0.878–0.913). Rating + 0.2 × score had the highest AUC, 0.908 (paired-bootstrap gain over rating +0.005 to +0.019), but only 13, 28 and 81 future 2400s in its top 50, 100 and 290, so it isn't used. At the very top of a list rating is still hard to beat: the deployed ranking's top 50, 100 and 290 held 18, 40 and 92 future 2400s, against 21, 40 and 89 for rating alone.
  • Who keeps competing. 37% of the 2024 cohort played no rated contest afterwards. Grouping players by contests played afterwards as well as by rating, the score's AUC falls to 0.613.
  • Caveats. The 2024 cohort had been explored in-sample before this test (traits chosen and scored on the same players: 0.655; from a 2023 start, three-year window: 0.668), so it is not a fully clean holdout. The first formula (rating, plus 0.6 × 12-month gain, plus a bonus for passing the climb filter) scored 0.879 overall, below rating alone.
  • Live list. Active players rated 2100 or more, with at most 60 followers, accounts under five years old and no olympiad-style or birth-year handles, drawn from the 1,200 highest by rating less a follower penalty. They must also pass filters the test didn't use, including 25 or more rated contests, a climb of 300 from their first 10 contests and a positive 12-month gain. The test covered ratings 1600–2300; 25 of the 64 now listed are above 2300. The AtCoder and Math Stack Exchange lists are not back-tested.
  • AUC = the probability that a randomly chosen player who reached 2400 is ranked above one who did not. Within-band AUC is averaged across bands, weighted by the number who reached 2400; a band needs at least 3 who did and 3 who didn't.

02

Contributors

Software engineers. The experts who run major open-source projects, the free software behind much of the internet such as Python and Rust, accepted their work, yet few people follow them online.

1,857people found so far. Experts accepted their work; few people follow them.
Method and numbers
  • Rules. 3 or more merged pull requests in the 12 months to 18 Oct 2026, no more than 40 per active month, and 150 GitHub followers or fewer. Removed: bots; anyone who has pressed merge in that project or another one searched; public members of major open-source organizations, including the project's own; people whose profile names a company or institution on a fixed list (large tech and chip companies, AI labs, trading firms, national labs, each project's sponsors, some universities); owners of a repository with 1,000 or more stars; and profiles that suggest a minor. All 2,929 candidates were checked; 1,072 were removed, each counted under the first matching reason: 547 for company or institution, 328 for followers, 135 for organization membership, 53 as committers elsewhere, 6 for a 1,000+ star repository, 3 as possible minors. 1,090 of the 1,857 have 12 or fewer followers; the median is 9.
  • Merge rights. The 1,685 outside Rust and mathlib have not pressed merge on any pull request in their project in the history fetched (since Oct 2023 for LLVM, CPython, Julia, JAX, NumPy, SciPy, DuckDB and llama.cpp; since Oct 2025 for the rest), and none is a public member of the project's organization. In Godot and Helix, where two or three people press merge for everyone, anyone who has approved someone else's pull request is removed too. A committer who pushes directly and keeps membership private would still slip through. Rust and mathlib merge through a bot, so for their 172 only organization membership is checked.
  • Why it isn't called prediction. In eight of the projects (not Rust or tinygrad), 2,653 people (2,721 person-project records) had merged pull requests between Oct 2023 and Oct 2024 and had not pressed merge or approved anyone else's. 156 pressed merge in the two years after, a sign they had been given write access; 135 of them in LLVM, so this is mostly an LLVM measurement. Merged-PR count ranks them with AUC 0.641, but that mostly separates one-time contributors from repeat ones: among records with 3 or more merged pull requests it is 0.556, and among those with 6 or more, as for 754 of the 1,857 listed and all 100 with reports, 0.518 (33 of 159 pressed merge, 28 in LLVM), which is chance. Other traits looked informative in-sample within count bands; the one out-of-sample check possible, split by project, had only 3 positives and went the other way (0.394, against 0.717 for count alone). So the list's order carries little information. What it offers is evidence of accepted work, for a person to read.

03

Dossiers

A short report on each person: what they built and where, with links to the proof. Written by software running on a laptop, and every pull request it cites is checked against the person’s real ones.

Method and numbers
  • Facts by code. Files touched, size, number of reviews, approval words in reviews (including “thanks” and “LGTM”), and core-code share: the share of changed files outside tests, docs, CI and examples. Extracted from GitHub, not written by the model.
  • Judgment by model. Specialty and summary come from Qwen3 8B running on a laptop. The dossier pipeline sends nothing about a candidate to an outside AI service.
  • Check. Every pull-request number the model cites, with or without “#”, is compared with the person's fetched pull requests: in 100 dossiers it flagged 2 numbers that are not the person's pull requests, and neither is linked: a line count (+1,307 lines), which the check now skips, and 50934, which is not one of their pull requests. It tests citations, not whether a summary is right, so a person reads the pull requests before anyone is contacted.
  • Not used. The model's difficulty ratings, because they don't separate people.
